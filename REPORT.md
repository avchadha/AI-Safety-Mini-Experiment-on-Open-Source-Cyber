# Open Weight Models and the Detection of Cyberattacks

Amrit Chadha

## I Research Question and Background

The growing use of AI in cybersecurity raises a question about how improvements in model capability affect the balance between offense and defense, particularly when a capable attacker can revise its behavior in response to detection. This experiment examines that relationship through a specific defensive task, asking whether open-weight models can recognize an AI-generated authorization attack from a completed session's service logs and a public description of the application's policy.

The study developed from an initial balanced benchmark on Lunary IDOR and Gradio path traversal, where medium and large defenders approached AUROC 1.000 and a rules baseline solved Gradio perfectly. Those results motivated a setting that would place greater pressure on detection, leading to the introduction of unusual authorized activity, repeated attacker revision, and an analysis of precision under rare attacks. The present experiment uses Lunary to examine the interaction between contextual recognition, transferred evasion, and the frequency of attacks in the population being screened.

All models were open-weight and accessed through Together AI's serverless API, with the broader experimental work costing approximately $10 against a roughly $15 budget. This choice keeps the study relevant to inexpensive hosted inference, and its findings suggest that practical defense depends on how a model interprets the available evidence and how its judgments are incorporated into an alerting system. Since the defenders judge completed sessions, the measured capability concerns retrospective recognition from a fixed record, making response timing and prevention questions for a later experiment.

## II Reasoning Behind the Design

Lunary's cross-tenant deletion vulnerability provides a useful test of contextual judgment because the offending request has an ordinary HTTP structure, with its malicious character arising from the relationship between the principal and the project being deleted. Each defender receives ordered access metadata together with a runbook containing the authorization policy and an episode-local ownership mapping, making it possible to assess whether the model connects an observed operation to the permissions governing it. Using a violation with an ordinary request shape makes that connection central to detection, since the request's appearance alone gives little basis for distinguishing it from an authorized deletion.

The public packet excludes request and response bodies, credentials, attacker instructions, and oracle labels, with a separate protected stream supplying the experiment's ground truth. Holding this boundary fixed gives every defender the same restricted evidence and prevents ground-truth labels from resolving the authorization question before the model has reasoned over the logs. A fresh isolated instance is used for each attempt, restoring the victim resource after a successful deletion and ensuring that successive revisions face the same objective.

Kimi K3 drives the attack through a constrained HTTP agent, receiving the objective and vulnerability-class brief and retaining three arms for each scenario. The baseline uses the standard attack instructions, the naive-stealth arm adds an instruction to conceal the activity, and the adaptive arm receives feedback from another invocation of Kimi K3 judging the public packet. This arrangement examines whether a capable model can use its own assessment of suspicious behavior to develop an attack that transfers to independent detectors.

The adaptive loop permits four revisions after the initial attempt, stops after a successful attack with proxy score below 0.5, and retains the successful attempt with the lowest proxy score. The instructions preserve the attack objective and logging infrastructure, so the selected episode must achieve the same unauthorized effect through behavior visible to the service. Kimi K3 is excluded from the evaluated defender set, whose scores are withheld throughout adaptation, giving the eventual replay an independent assessment of how the selected behavior appears to other models.

The benign corpus contains 310 scripted sessions, including normal workflows, bulk deletion of owned projects, repeated errors followed by retries, and high-volume enumeration of owned resources. These hard negatives make the operational policy relevant to the detector's decision, since deletion frequency or request volume by itself would also flag legitimate activity. Their inclusion therefore tests whether a model can use the authorization context to maintain sensitivity to attacks across a more demanding collection of benign behavior.

The five defenders span Qwen3.5-9B, gpt-oss-20b, Llama-3.3-70B, gpt-oss-120b, and DeepSeek-V4-Pro, covering several families and architectures under inexpensive inference settings. Qwen and DeepSeek use disabled thinking, gpt-oss uses low reasoning effort, and Llama is non-reasoning, making the comparison informative about the configurations available in this deployment regime. Establishing a causal relationship between parameter count and detection would require controlling the training, architecture, reasoning budget, and serving differences that accompany the models in this set.

The dataset contains 24 matched scenarios, divided by index into four calibration and 20 test pairs, with three retained arms per scenario producing 72 attack episodes. Combining these with the benign corpus gives 382 episodes and 1,910 defender judgments, and freezing the packets before replay keeps the evidence constant across models. Each defender's threshold is selected to keep empirical FPR at or below 10% on the benign corpus, with baseline and evasive recall then measured on the 20 test pairs, placing the model comparison under a common constraint on false positives.

## III High Level Findings

The following table summarizes detection at the selected thresholds and projected precision at one attack per 1,000 sessions, using evasive recall for the prevalence calculation. Transfer is the evaluated defender's score reduction divided by the corresponding proxy reduction, averaged across pairs with proxy improvement of at least 0.05, which stabilizes the denominator and leaves 19 eligible scenarios comprising three calibration and 16 test pairs. The transfer intervals come from a paired bootstrap, so their pooled exploratory scope accompanies the held-out recall results.

| Defender | FPR | Baseline recall | Evasive recall | Transfer τ [95% CI] | Precision at 1 in 1,000 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- | ---: |
| gpt-oss-20b | 6.77% | 0.95 | 0.80 | 0.282 [0.002, 0.690] | 1.168% |
| gpt-oss-120b | 6.77% | 0.65 | 0.30 | 0.533 [0.298, 0.798] | 0.441% |
| DeepSeek-V4-Pro | 5.16% | 0.50 | 0.60 | 0.062 [-0.060, 0.215] | 1.150% |
| Qwen3.5-9B | 9.35% | 0.10 | 0.05 | -0.147 [-0.530, 0.257] | 0.053% |
| Llama-3.3-70B | 3.87% | 0.05 | 0.05 | -0.021 [-0.165, 0.122] | 0.129% |

The strongest baseline detector is gpt-oss-20b, which identifies 19 of 20 test attacks and retains detection of 16 after adaptation, and the most pronounced loss occurs for gpt-oss-120b, whose detected count falls from 13 to six. DeepSeek detects 10 baseline and 12 evasive attacks, with a transfer interval spanning zero, and Qwen and Llama's low starting recall limits what their small or negative transfer values can establish about effective robustness. Choosing a defender therefore requires considering its initial attack recognition alongside its sensitivity to adaptation, since a small change under evasion can accompany a model that already misses most attacks.

Across all 24 scenarios, the mean proxy-score decrease from baseline to evasive is 0.248, with a 95% interval of [0.176, 0.316], and the corresponding baseline-to-naive-stealth decrease is -0.004, with interval [-0.021, 0.012]. The recorded median HTTP counts are four for baseline, 11 for evasive, and two for naive-stealth, associating the adaptive procedure's score reduction with more surrounding activity in its retained episodes, all of which achieve the unauthorized deletion.

## IV Examination of the Findings

The model ranking suggests that detection depends substantially on how the model responds to the specific evidence presented, with gpt-oss-20b performing best and the largest dense model, Llama-3.3-70B, detecting a single baseline attack. The larger gpt-oss model also performs below the smaller one under the recorded settings, giving parameter count limited value as a deployment criterion in this experiment. Selecting a defender on demonstrated performance in the intended task would therefore have produced a more reliable choice within the tested set.

A plausible explanation is that the relevant training and inference behavior affect whether the model applies the runbook's ownership relation consistently, since the decisive information requires connecting a principal, a resource, and an operation across the packet. A model could recognize the general appearance of suspicious activity and give insufficient weight to that relation when assigning a score, making its judgment sensitive to changes in surrounding behavior. Testing this explanation would require examining which events influence its judgment and varying the supplied authorization context, with a controlled family comparison helping establish how additional capacity interacts with the same interpretive task.

The transfer result gives the attacker's adaptation a broader significance because a procedure guided by one model reduces detection by an evaluated model whose judgments were withheld. This demonstrates that some changes to the attacker's observable behavior influence multiple detectors, and the variation across defenders suggests that susceptibility depends on how those detectors aggregate and interpret the session. Consequently, baseline recognition and resistance to this form of transferred evasion need separate evaluation when choosing a model for operational use.

The increase in request counts supports behavioral padding as a possible explanation for the reduced scores, since additional authorized-looking activity can change the composition of the session surrounding the violating deletion. If a detector's judgment depends heavily on the overall character of the session, that surrounding activity could reduce the weight assigned to a decisive policy violation. The observation motivates an intervention that changes the surrounding traffic and preserves the unauthorized operation and its ownership context, allowing the proposed mechanism to be examined directly.

The naive-stealth arm's score change close to zero suggests that an instruction to conceal the attack provides limited guidance about which observable behavior to change. Feedback supplies a judgment of each attempt and gives the attacker a way to explore what its scoring model regards as suspicious, potentially helping it translate the goal of evasion into concrete revisions. Selection also contributes to the observed proxy improvement because the adaptive procedure retains the lowest-scoring successful attempt from a sequence that includes baseline. Feedback, additional attempts, and selection all form part of the procedure whose effect is measured, and their individual contributions remain open to examination. A control with a matched attempt budget and the same proxy-based selection rule would help determine how much of the gain comes from using the proxy's rationale to revise behavior, informing whether improved attack reasoning or additional search drives the observed result.

The nature of the target gives this question an interpretable basis because successful deletion remains in the log and the runbook supplies the relationship needed to judge its authorization. The attacker's allowed revisions can change surrounding behavior and detector confidence, leaving the decisive operation available for recognition throughout the comparison. Failures under these conditions suggest that present evidence can receive insufficient weight, making contextual interpretation and the treatment of session composition useful subjects for further defense research.

The prevalence calculation then shows how the measured detection behavior would enter an alerting system, using evasive recall $r$, benign FPR $f$, and attack prevalence $p$:

$$
\operatorname{Precision}(p)=\frac{rp}{rp+f(1-p)}.
$$

At one attack per 1,000 sessions, gpt-oss-20b would produce 80 true alerts and approximately 6,767 false alerts per 100,000 sessions, placing its precision at about 1.17%. DeepSeek's lower false-positive rate and lower evasive recall produce precision of about 1.15%, giving both models a substantial review burden under this hypothetical prevalence. The calculation explains how a detector that recognizes most benchmark attacks can generate a stream of alerts dominated by legitimate activity when deployed over a predominantly benign population.

At the same prevalence and recall of 0.80, achieving 50% precision would require FPR of approximately 0.08%, and holding the measured gpt-oss-20b FPR fixed would leave precision near 1.5% with perfect recall. This places a concrete demand on the operating point used for rare-attack screening, since improvements in recall at the existing FPR would leave the alert stream heavily populated by false positives. The experiment therefore makes independent validation at much lower FPRs central to establishing whether the model's recognition ability can become useful in that setting.

A stricter threshold could reduce the alert volume, with its value depending on how much attack recall survives the change, and a pre-filter could raise prevalence among the sessions reaching the model by screening out enough benign traffic. A cascade could then assign more expensive reasoning or additional evidence to the smaller set of escalated sessions, making inference resources responsive to the uncertainty of the case. Evaluating these approaches would require measuring recall across the entire pipeline, because each stage's selection determines which attacks reach the subsequent stage.

The recorded token ledger gives approximately $0.16 per retained successful evasive scenario and $0.00067 per screened session, making inference inexpensive at the unit of a single judgment. Offense accounting includes the retained arms and omits discarded revisions and proxy calls, and the defense estimate uses blended prices, so these figures provide a partial view of experimental cost. Their practical interpretation depends on the volume of screening and follow-up work, since a deployment may process thousands of benign sessions for each attack and allocate human or automated verification to the resulting alerts.

## V Scope and Further Research

The sample size places the findings within an exploratory regime, with each additional detected attack moving test recall by five percentage points and the transfer analysis using 19 pairs conditioned on measurable proxy improvement. The large difference between the strongest and weakest baseline detectors gives the study a useful empirical result, and smaller effects would benefit from additional scenarios and seeds. Separate uncertainty estimates for recall, false positives, and transferred score reduction would also clarify which parts of the apparent ordering persist as the sample grows.

The benign corpus creates a further constraint on deployment interpretation because its scripted workflows cover a limited range of authorized behavior, and the same sessions select the threshold and supply the reported FPR. A single false-positive session represents about 0.32% of this corpus, placing the 0.08% operating point motivated by the prevalence calculation below the resolution of its observed error counts. A larger independent benign corpus would test whether the operating point generalizes to new workflows and whether sufficiently low false-positive rates can be estimated with useful precision. The assumed prevalence likewise belongs to the deployment scenario, making the base-rate projections conditional on the measured recall and FPR remaining applicable in the population being screened.

Replication on another application would examine how the conclusions depend on the supplied ownership mapping and the visibility of the harmful operation, especially for attacks whose consequences emerge over several stages or sessions. The explicit policy context is an advantage of this benchmark, and evaluating deployments would require determining which equivalent authorization evidence the monitor can obtain and how reliably it can connect that evidence to logged activity. This would bring the task closer to practical defense by incorporating the availability of context into the assessment of model performance.

Reasoning and serving choices also define the scope of the model comparison, with dated provider aliases recording the observed configurations and larger reasoning budgets providing a separate experimental variable. Parse failures are retained at probability 0.5 and fall below every selected threshold, making the measured recall reflect the full prediction pipeline, including its ability to return usable judgments. Studying these effects alongside within-family scaling would help identify whether performance gains arise from model capacity, task-relevant training, or additional computation at inference.

The findings connect model choice to how an alert will be used, since a defender needs to recognize the violating operation, retain that recognition under revised behavior, and produce a volume of alerts that the surrounding process can handle. A useful continuation would combine independent benign validation with interventions on attacker behavior and the context available to defenders, allowing the reasons for successful recognition and evasion to be tested directly. Extending that work to cross-session evidence and interactive defense would connect the present observations to when an attack can be recognized and what action can follow from that recognition.
