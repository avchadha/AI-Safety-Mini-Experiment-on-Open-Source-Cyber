# Open Weight Models and the Detection of Cyberattacks

Amrit Chadha

## I Research Question and Background

Improvements in AI's ability to carry out cyberattacks raise a question about what a defender needs to recognize them, especially when an attacker can revise its behavior in response to a model's judgment. A successful attack may leave enough evidence for its authorization violation to be reconstructed, making the defender's ability to interpret that evidence central to the balance between offense and defense. This experiment examines that ability by asking whether open-weight models can identify an AI-generated attack from a completed session's service logs and a public description of the application's policy. Its findings connect the quality of that judgment to the behavior the attacker chooses and the population in which the detector will be used.

The study began with a balanced benchmark on Lunary IDOR and Gradio path traversal, where medium and large defenders approached AUROC 1.000 and a rules baseline solved Gradio perfectly. Such results give reason to examine what made recognition straightforward, since a benchmark with clearly separated legitimate and malicious behavior can reward patterns whose usefulness depends on that separation. The present experiment therefore introduces unusual authorized activity, repeated attacker revision, and rare-attack prevalence, using Lunary to examine how these conditions affect detection. All models were accessed through Together AI's serverless API, with the broader work costing approximately $10 against a roughly $15 budget, placing the findings in the context of inexpensive hosted inference. These judgments concern completed sessions, making the availability of the same information during an attack an additional condition for extending recognition into intervention.

## II Reasoning Behind the Design

Lunary's cross-tenant deletion vulnerability makes the authorization relationship itself important to detection, since the offending request has the ordinary structure of a project deletion. The defender receives ordered access metadata and a runbook giving the policy and an episode-local ownership mapping, allowing it to connect the principal making a request to the project affected by it. For example, deleting several projects can be legitimate when the principal owns them, and deleting a single project can violate the policy when it belongs to another tenant. These cases make the choice of target useful for examining whether the model applies the supplied relationship consistently, since the appearance of the request gives little basis for resolving its authorization.

The public packet excludes request and response bodies, credentials, attacker instructions, and oracle labels, and a separate protected stream supplies the ground truth. This boundary preserves the reasoning the defender must perform over the logs, because giving it the authorization verdict would settle the question being evaluated. Each attempt also uses a fresh isolated instance, restoring the victim resource after a successful deletion so that successive revisions face the same objective. The benign corpus contains 310 scripted sessions, including ordinary workflows, bulk deletion of owned projects, repeated errors followed by retries, and high-volume enumeration of owned resources. Incorporating these hard negatives places legitimate behavior within the range of activity that could superficially appear suspicious, requiring the defender to use the policy context when judging destructive operations and high request volume.

Kimi K3 conducts the attack through a constrained HTTP agent given the objective and vulnerability-class brief, with three arms retained for each scenario. The baseline uses standard attack instructions, the naive-stealth arm adds an instruction to conceal the activity, and the adaptive arm receives feedback from another invocation of Kimi K3 judging the public packet. The adaptive procedure permits four revisions after the initial attempt, stops after a successful attack with proxy score below 0.5, and retains the successful attempt with the lowest proxy score. The objective and logging infrastructure remain fixed throughout revision, requiring the selected behavior to achieve the same unauthorized effect through requests visible to the service. Kimi K3 is excluded from the evaluated defenders, whose judgments are withheld during adaptation, so replay examines how revisions guided by one model affect the recognition of independent models.

The five defenders are Qwen3.5-9B, gpt-oss-20b, Llama-3.3-70B, gpt-oss-120b, and DeepSeek-V4-Pro, covering several families and architectures under inexpensive inference settings. Qwen and DeepSeek use disabled thinking, gpt-oss uses low reasoning effort, and Llama is non-reasoning, making the results applicable to those recorded configurations. The comparison can establish which configuration performs best on the task, and attributing that ordering to parameter count would require a further argument accounting for training, architecture, reasoning budget, and serving differences. Treating those differences as part of the comparison keeps the empirical model ranking tied to the choices actually available to a user of the hosted service.

The dataset contains 24 matched scenarios, divided by index into four calibration and 20 test pairs, with the three arms producing 72 attack episodes. Together with the benign corpus, these yield 382 episodes and 1,910 defender judgments, and freezing the packets before replay ensures that every model judges the same evidence. Each threshold is selected to keep empirical FPR at or below 10% on the benign corpus, after which baseline and evasive recall are measured on the 20 test pairs. This provides a common false-positive constraint for comparing attack recognition, and the prevalence analysis then examines whether the resulting operating points could support useful screening when attacks are rare.

## III High Level Findings

The table reports detection at the selected thresholds and projected precision at one attack per 1,000 sessions, using evasive recall in the prevalence calculation. Transfer is the evaluated defender's score reduction divided by the corresponding proxy reduction, averaged across pairs with proxy improvement of at least 0.05. This requirement stabilizes the denominator and leaves 19 eligible scenarios, comprising three calibration and 16 test pairs, with paired bootstrap intervals describing a pooled exploratory analysis alongside recall measured on the held-out test pairs.

The strongest baseline detector, gpt-oss-20b, recognizes 19 of 20 test attacks and retains detection of 16 after adaptation, and the greatest decline occurs for gpt-oss-120b, whose detected count falls from 13 to six. DeepSeek recognizes 10 baseline and 12 evasive attacks, with a transfer interval spanning zero, and Qwen and Llama begin with recall of 0.10 and 0.05. The latter results make their small or negative transfer values difficult to interpret as useful robustness, since a detector that already misses most attacks has little recognition to preserve under adaptation.

Across all 24 scenarios, the mean proxy-score decrease from baseline to evasive is 0.248, with a 95% interval of [0.176, 0.316], and the baseline-to-naive-stealth decrease is -0.004, with interval [-0.021, 0.012]. Recorded median HTTP counts are four for baseline, 11 for evasive, and two for naive-stealth, associating adaptive score reduction with an increase in surrounding activity. All retained evasive episodes achieve the unauthorized deletion, so the observed reduction in suspicion accompanies preservation of the attack's objective and the operation that establishes its success.

## IV Examination of the Findings

The ranking challenges the assumption that a model's parameter count gives a reliable basis for choosing it as a defender, since gpt-oss-20b performs best and Llama-3.3-70B recognizes a single baseline attack. The larger gpt-oss model also performs below the smaller one, making the same assumption questionable within that family under the recorded settings. A plausible explanation is that training and inference behavior affect how consistently a model connects the principal, resource, and operation using the runbook's ownership relation. If a model judges a session through its general resemblance to suspicious activity, its decision may depend heavily on features whose relevance to the authorization question is limited. Since those influences vary across the tested configurations, the ordering supports a choice among them and leaves the causal contribution of additional capacity open to examination.

The transfer result extends this concern to an attacker that can revise its observable behavior, because feedback from one model leads to reduced recognition by evaluated defenders whose judgments were withheld. This gives the adaptation significance beyond the scoring proxy, suggesting that some revisions influence features to which multiple models respond. The variation across defenders also matters to the interpretation, since the decline for gpt-oss-120b accompanies a near-zero mean transfer estimate for DeepSeek and low starting recall for Qwen and Llama. A small transfer estimate gains practical value when the model also recognizes the attacks presented to it, and DeepSeek's additional two detections and interval spanning zero leave the explanation for its response open. Consequently, choosing a defender requires examining its initial recognition and what happens to that recognition under adaptation, with each result informing a different part of the deployment decision.

The increase in request counts makes behavioral padding a plausible explanation for evasion, since additional authorized-looking requests alter the activity surrounding the violating deletion. The importance of this possibility follows from the policy itself, because an unauthorized deletion remains a violation when legitimate operations accompany it. If the model's judgment of that deletion changes as ordinary activity is added, the judgment has become sensitive to information with limited bearing on who owns the resource. In this way, padding could exploit the model's tendency to summarize the character of a session when the decisive evidence concerns a particular operation. The counts establish an association that warrants examination, and an intervention varying surrounding traffic and preserving the deletion and ownership relation would test whether that association reflects the proposed mechanism. Such a test would also clarify whether directing attention to individual policy violations helps a defender preserve recognition across changes in session composition.

The naive-stealth arm's score change close to zero further suggests that concealment needs to be translated into a concrete change in behavior before it can affect the model's judgment. Feedback gives the attacker information about how an attempt appears to its scoring model, potentially helping it connect the goal of evasion to revisions of the public record. The adaptive procedure also retains the lowest-scoring successful attempt from a sequence including baseline, so the observed improvement incorporates feedback, additional search, and selection. Attributing that improvement to feedback requires accounting for the opportunity to try several attacks and choose the one judged least suspicious. A control could use the same attempt budget to generate independent attacks from the baseline instructions and select the successful attempt with the lowest proxy score, giving repeated search a comparable opportunity to improve the retained result. Comparing that outcome to adaptive revision would clarify how much of the observed improvement comes from using the proxy's rationale to change behavior.

The prevalence calculation exposes a further assumption in the move from benchmark performance to useful defense, since the proportion of attacks in the screened population determines how many alerts represent real violations. Applying evasive recall $r$ and benign FPR $f$ to a population with attack prevalence $p$ makes this dependence explicit through the projected precision:

$$
\operatorname{Precision}(p)=\frac{rp}{rp+f(1-p)}.
$$

At one attack per 1,000 sessions, gpt-oss-20b would produce 80 true alerts and approximately 6,767 false alerts per 100,000 sessions, giving precision of about 1.17%. DeepSeek's lower FPR and lower evasive recall produce precision of about 1.15%, showing how both configurations would place substantial demands on review under this assumed prevalence. A system can recognize most of the attacks in a benchmark and generate an alert stream dominated by legitimate activity, because the large benign population repeatedly contributes false positives. The useful interpretation of recall therefore depends on the setting in which the judgment will become an alert and the capacity available to investigate it.

This demand becomes clearer by asking what operating point would produce alerts that justify their volume, with 50% precision at the same prevalence and recall of 0.80 requiring FPR of approximately 0.08%. Holding gpt-oss-20b's measured FPR fixed would leave precision near 1.5% with perfect recall, placing the improvement needed for rare-attack screening primarily in the treatment of benign traffic. A stricter threshold could reduce false positives, and its usefulness would depend on the attack recognition retained at that threshold. A pre-filter could also raise prevalence among the sessions reaching the model, after which a cascade could assign additional evidence or reasoning to uncertain cases. Each stage would need evaluation as part of the complete system, since screening determines which attacks remain available for later recognition and escalation.

The recorded token ledger gives approximately $0.16 per retained successful evasive scenario and $0.00067 per screened session, making a single model judgment inexpensive in this experiment. Offense accounting includes the retained arms and omits discarded revisions and proxy calls, and defense accounting uses blended prices, so the ledger gives a partial estimate of the resources used. Its relevance to deployment also depends on how often each activity occurs, since screening can involve thousands of benign sessions for each attack and further work on every generated alert. Cheap inference can make broad screening feasible, and the prevalence calculation shows why its value must be assessed together with the effort required to decide which alerts merit action.

## V Scope and Further Research

The sample size limits how confidently the model ordering and transfer effects can be generalized, since each additional detected test attack moves recall by five percentage points and transfer uses 19 pairs conditioned on measurable proxy improvement. The large gap between the strongest and weakest baseline detectors gives reason to take model choice seriously in this task, and smaller changes would benefit from further scenarios and seeds. Separate uncertainty estimates for recall, FPR, and transferred score reduction would clarify how much of the observed ordering persists across repeated collection. The explicit ownership mapping also shapes the scope of the result, making replication on another application useful for examining what recognition requires when the harmful effect emerges over several stages or sessions.

The benign corpus places an additional limit on the deployment projections because its scripted workflows cover a restricted range of authorized behavior, and the same sessions select the thresholds and supply the reported FPRs. A single false-positive session represents about 0.32% of this corpus, putting the 0.08% operating point motivated by rare-attack screening below the resolution of its observed error counts. Establishing performance at that operating point would require a substantially larger independent benign corpus, with enough varied workflows to examine whether the apparent errors reflect behavior encountered in practice. The assumed prevalence also belongs to the deployment setting, so carrying the projection into that setting requires evidence that the measured recall and FPR remain applicable to its traffic.

The available authorization context and inference configuration are likewise part of what makes the benchmark's judgments possible, since the runbook supplies ownership information and the serving settings determine how the model processes it. A deployed monitor would need a reliable way to obtain equivalent evidence and connect it to logged activity, making the availability of context part of the question of whether detection will succeed. Dated provider aliases record the observed configurations, and larger reasoning budgets provide another variable to examine alongside training and within-family scaling. Parse failures are retained at probability 0.5 and fall below every selected threshold, so the results also incorporate the prediction pipeline's ability to return a usable judgment.

The experiment shows that an inexpensive open-weight model can recognize most attacks in this benchmark, and that transferred evasion and rare-attack prevalence constrain the usefulness of that recognition. Establishing useful defense therefore requires examining how a model reaches its judgment and how that judgment becomes an alert, making independent benign validation and interventions on attacker behavior and defender context natural continuations of the study. Extending these tests to cross-session evidence and interactive defense would connect the quality of the model's interpretation to when a violation can be recognized and how that recognition supports a response.
